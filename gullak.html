Self-directed case study

A savings product for women the banking system doesn't reach

Rural women want to save. Most can't. Not because they lack the will, but because every product on offer assumes a bank branch nearby, a predictable income, and money you won't need back suddenly. None of those hold.

So I gave myself the problem: design something that works for someone earning ₹40 on a good day, four kilometres from the nearest ATM. This is the model I arrived at.

Type — Product case study, self-directed Scope — Discovery to roadmap and metrics Year — 2025

The starting point

What I assumed, and why it matters

Every one of these constrains the design. If any turns out false, parts of the model collapse — which is exactly why they're written down first rather than discovered later.

  • Income is small and irregular. Not ₹5,000 a month. ₹20 today, nothing tomorrow, ₹150 on market day. Any product with a fixed monthly commitment fails on day one.
  • The bank is far. No branch, no ATM within practical reach. A trip costs a day's earnings in time and fare, so it has to be worth making — which small savings never are.
  • SHG women are the trust layer. Self-Help Group members are already trusted, already handle group money, already meet regularly. That social infrastructure exists; the product should ride it rather than rebuild it.
  • Phones are basic, and confidence is lower than capability. Most have a phone. Fewer will risk using it for money without someone beside them the first few times.
  • The product cannot hold the money. Deposits sit with an RBI-regulated bank; this is a front-end. Anything else is both illegal and untrustworthy, and the two are related.

The insight

Borrow the delivery model, not the banking model

Quick commerce solved a version of this problem: get something to someone's door, fast, in places infrastructure doesn't reach. Microfinance solved a different one: extend trust where there's no collateral or credit history. Neither works alone here. Together they do.

Combine the speed of a quick-commerce network with the trust of a microfinance institution — and put both in the hands of a woman the village already knows.The product pitch, in one line
AgentsTwo women per village, drawn from the local SHG. They collect deposits (₹10–₹200), record them in an offline-capable app, and hand out cash on withdrawal. A portion of their own money is locked as security, which makes them guarantors rather than employees.
FloatA cash reserve held in the village — around ₹10,000 — so a withdrawal doesn't require a journey. The float is what makes the promise of instant access real rather than aspirational.
ClusterSix to eight neighbouring villages grouped together. Close enough that an agent can physically reach another within the day, which is the precondition for the whole fallback system working.
Peer borrowingWhen one village's float runs dry, its agent requests cash from another agent in the cluster through the app. Liquidity is pooled socially before it's pooled institutionally.
HubA partner bank branch or post office anchoring each cluster. Excess deposits go here for safekeeping; emergency replenishment comes back out.
ExecutiveOne per cluster. Refills floats weekly, collects surplus, delivers emergency cash, trains agents, and helps women through the paperwork for insurance, pension and government schemes. The bridge between the village and formal banking.

The core mechanic

Three levels of fallback on a withdrawal

Deposits are the easy half — someone comes to your door and takes your money. Withdrawals are where trust is won or lost, because the request usually arrives during an emergency. Savitri needs ₹1,500 today, for a hospital. Here is what happens.

The agent's float covers itShe has the cash in the village. Savitri has her money in minutes, and never learns a fallback system exists. This is the path most requests should take.
Peer borrowing across the clusterThe agent has only ₹500. Through the app she requests the remaining ₹1,000 from an agent in a neighbouring village, who delivers it the same day. The network covers what the village couldn't.
Executive delivery from the hubEvery agent in the cluster is short. The executive collects cash from the partner bank or post office and rides it out within hours. Slower, and rare by design — but it means the answer is never no.

The deposit journey is deliberately unremarkable by comparison: Savitri hands over ₹20, the agent records it, and an SMS in her own language arrives alongside a passbook update. Two confirmations, one paper and one digital, because trust is being built and one of them is a format she already believes.

What's actually broken

Five gaps

TrustFormal banks and apps feel like places where money goes in and questions come back out.
AccessNo branch, no ATM. Distance turns a small deposit into an expensive errand.
FlexibilityFixed-amount, fixed-date products assume an income these women don't have.
EmergencySavings you can't reach today are indistinguishable from savings you don't have.
KnowledgeSchemes and entitlements exist that nobody has explained, in a language and format that works.

Sequencing

What ships when

The MVP proves one thing only: that money can go in and come back out of a village reliably. Everything else — digital literacy, credit, insurance — is earned on top of that proof, and pointless without it.

Phase 1 · MVP

Prove the loop

  • Two agents per village
  • ₹10,000 float per village
  • Peer borrowing within the cluster
  • Direct deposit to the hub
  • Offline-capable app with SMS receipts
Phase 2 · Scale and teach

Add the human layer

  • Executives covering 6–8 villages
  • Voice-enabled dashboards in local language
  • Financial literacy campaigns
  • Three-month saving streak unlocks micro-insurance
Phase 3 · Financial identity

Turn savings into standing

  • Micro-loans
  • Credit scoring from savings history
  • Government scheme integration
  • Digital transfers, reducing physical cash

Measurement

What I'd watch, and in what order

One north star, three supporting layers. The ordering matters more than the list — growth numbers look healthy long after the thing that mattered has stopped working.

North star
Total savings collected. The only number that means the product did the thing it exists to do. Everything else is diagnostic.
Behaviour
Monthly deposits per saver, and streaks at three, six and twelve months. Saving once is a trial; saving on a rhythm is a habit, and only the second one compounds.
Impact
Emergency withdrawals fulfilled — and at which fallback level. If level three fires often, the float sizing is wrong. Plus insurance coverage reached through streaks.
Growth
Ninety-day retention and referrals. In a village, referral is not a marketing metric — it's the clearest available signal that the product is trusted.

Open questions I'd want to answer before building. The unit economics of holding idle float in every village, and whether the withdrawal speed it buys is worth the cost. How agent fraud is detected when the same person records the transaction and holds the cash. What the regulatory posture actually is for cash-in-hand collection ahead of the bank leg settling. And whether the executive role scales, or quietly becomes the bottleneck the whole cluster depends on.

Happy to talk through any of this

Including the parts I'd change. This was written to practise the discovery-to-metrics arc end to end, and the open questions above are the honest state of it.
