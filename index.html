How I work

Product decisions, expressed as rules the system enforces

Discovery from real usage

Most of my work starts as a user report — "my bill says the wrong number" — that I reproduce against real data, trace to root cause, and turn into a written spec. The bug report is the research.

Rules over conventions

A guard-filled walk-in must never consume a resident's pass. Credit cannot coexist with an outstanding due. "Paid" is a status only the payment gateway can set, never a human. These are product decisions written as invariants.

Sequencing over speed

Three apps on one API means migration order, backward compatibility, and release timing are the actual design problem. Most of my hard calls are about rollout, not features.

Four personas, one dataset

Resident, committee member, gate staff, broker — each needs a different surface over the same underlying data, with different permissions and a different definition of "done".

Hisaab — bookkeeping for shops that have no time for bookkeeping

Live ↗

The shopkeeper already has the data. It's in the bank SMS on their phone, in the WhatsApp order they just read out, in the photo of a bill in their gallery. Books don't get kept because every app asks them to type all of it in a second time.

So Hisaab reads rather than asks: bank and UPI messages parsed on the device, bills read from a photo, WhatsApp orders priced against the shop's own list. Udhaar tracked with reminders and a UPI link so the customer can simply pay. Staff sign in and see only the orders they handle. SMS and bill photos are read on the phone and never uploaded — the privacy position is the product, not a footnote.

Gullak — a savings product for women the banking system doesn't reach

Read the case study →

How do you design savings for someone earning ₹40 on a good day, four kilometres from the nearest ATM? Fixed-deposit products assume an income she doesn't have, and savings she can't reach in an emergency are indistinguishable from savings she doesn't have.

My answer borrows the delivery model of quick commerce and the trust model of microfinance: two SHG women per village holding a cash float, a cluster of villages that can lend liquidity to each other, and a three-level fallback so the answer to a withdrawal request is never no.
